How to use the vitamin D calculator

Choose the US, UK or European recommendation and type your age. You get the recommended amount and the upper limit at once, in IU and in mcg.

Then add what you take: type the amount on the label for one capsule, drop or tablet, pick IU or mcg, and say how many you take a day or a week. Add as many supplements as you like; the calculator adds them up and shows the share of the recommended amount and of the upper limit.

Sources

  • US recommended amounts and upper limits: MedlinePlus (US National Library of Medicine), Vitamin D, reviewed 21 January 2025 (read 2026-11-07): adequate intake 400 IU (10 mcg) from birth to 12 months; RDA 600 IU (15 mcg) from 1 to 70 years, in pregnancy and while breastfeeding; 800 IU (20 mcg) over 70; upper limits 1,000 IU (25 mcg) at 0–6 months, 1,500 IU (38 mcg) at 7–12 months, 2,500 IU (63 mcg) at 1–3 years, 3,000 IU (75 mcg) at 4–8 years and 4,000 IU (100 mcg) from 9 years. The mcg values for the upper limits are rounded in the source. The same RDA values appear on the Harvard T.H. Chan Nutrition Source (last reviewed March 2023) and the Linus Pauling Institute micronutrient page.
  • UK advice: NHS, Vitamin D (reviewed 3 August 2020, read 2026-11-07): 10 micrograms (400 IU) a day for children from 1 year and adults; 8.5–10 micrograms for babies up to 1 year; do not take more than 100 micrograms (4,000 IU) a day from age 11, 50 micrograms (2,000 IU) at ages 1–10 and 25 micrograms (1,000 IU) under 12 months. The IU for the baby range (340–400) is worked out here with the same factor.
  • EU values: EFSA (European Food Safety Authority). Adequate intakes from the EFSA scientific opinion on dietary reference values for vitamin D (2016; abstract read on the University of Dundee repository record, 2026-11-07): 15 µg a day for adults and for children aged 1–17, 10 µg a day for infants aged 7–11 months, and 15 µg a day in pregnancy and lactation; EFSA sets no adequate intake for younger infants in that abstract. Upper limits from the EFSA overview of tolerable upper intake levels (version 1.1, August 2025, citing the EFSA NDA Panel 2018 and 2023 opinions; read 2026-11-07): 25 µg a day at 0–6 months, 35 µg at 7–11 months, 50 µg at 1–10 years and 100 µg from 11 years, in adults, pregnancy and lactation. The EFSA topic page confirms the 100 µg adult limit was kept in August 2023. The IU for EU values are worked out here with the factor of 40.
  • IU and mcg: the pages above always list the two together (10 mcg = 400 IU, 15 mcg = 600 IU, 20 mcg = 800 IU, 25 mcg = 1,000 IU, 100 mcg = 4,000 IU), which is a factor of 40. No page I could open states the rule in words, so the factor is implied by those pairs; the US FDA guidance page returned an error and the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements site refused the request.
  • Blood level units: 1 ng/mL = 2.5 nmol/L, from the pairs 20 ng/mL = 50 nmol/L (Omnicalculator) and 40–60 ng/mL = 100–150 nmol/L (GrassrootsHealth), both read 2026-11-07. This is a unit conversion only; the page gives no verdict on a level.

Common questions

How many IU is 1 mcg of vitamin D?

1 mcg of vitamin D is 40 IU, so IU ÷ 40 = mcg and mcg × 40 = IU. 1,000 IU = 25 mcg, 2,000 IU = 50 mcg and 5,000 IU = 125 mcg. The official pages list the two together (for example 600 IU (15 mcg)), which is a factor of 40.

How much vitamin D do I need a day?

MedlinePlus lists 600 IU (15 mcg) a day for ages 1 to 70, 800 IU (20 mcg) over 70, and 400 IU (10 mcg) for babies. The NHS advises 10 micrograms (400 IU) a day from age 1. EFSA sets an adequate intake of 15 micrograms (600 IU) a day for adults and children from 1 to 17. The calculator shows the one you choose for your age.

What is the upper limit for vitamin D?

For ages 9 and over the upper limit is 4,000 IU (100 mcg) a day in the US; the NHS gives the same 100 micrograms from age 11, 50 micrograms (2,000 IU) at ages 1–10 and 25 micrograms (1,000 IU) under 12 months, and EFSA gives 100 micrograms from age 11, 50 micrograms at ages 1–10, 35 micrograms at 7–11 months and 25 micrograms under 7 months. It is the highest daily amount not expected to cause harm in most people.

Is 5,000 IU of vitamin D too much?

5,000 IU is 125 mcg, above the 4,000 IU (100 mcg) upper limit for ages 9 and over. If a doctor prescribed it, follow that advice; if you chose it yourself, ask a clinician. The calculator shows the share of the upper limit for what you take.

How do I convert a weekly vitamin D dose to a daily amount?

Divide by 7. A 50,000 IU capsule once a week is 7,143 IU (178.6 mcg) a day on average. Choose “a week” next to how many you take and the calculator does it, then compares the daily average with the upper limit. The upper limit is a daily figure, so ask your clinician about a prescribed weekly dose.

What is the difference between IU and mcg?

IU (international units) and mcg (micrograms) are two ways to write the same amount of vitamin D: IU is based on biological effect and mcg on weight. Labels in the US now list micrograms, often with IU in brackets.

How do I convert ng/mL to nmol/L?

Multiply ng/mL by 2.5: 20 ng/mL = 50 nmol/L and 40 ng/mL = 100 nmol/L. Open Go advanced to convert a blood test result either way. The page does not say what a level means; ask your clinician.

Should I take a vitamin D supplement?

The NHS advises everyone to consider 10 micrograms a day in autumn and winter, and all year for people who rarely go outdoors, cover most of their skin outdoors or have dark skin. That is UK advice; ask a clinician what suits you.
