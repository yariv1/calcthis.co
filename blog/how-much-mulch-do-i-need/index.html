How Much Mulch Do I Need?

Ordering too little means a second trip to the garden center. Ordering too much leaves a pile in your driveway. A two-minute calculation before you buy gets you the right amount the first time.

The Formula

Mulch volume is the same calculation every time: area times depth, then convert to cubic yards (how most suppliers sell bulk mulch) or cubic feet (how bagged mulch is labeled).

Length (ft) × Width (ft) × Depth (ft) ÷ 27 = Cubic yards Depth in feet — divide inches by 12 first. ÷27 converts cubic feet to cubic yards.

A typical front-yard bed 20 ft long and 4 ft wide, mulched 3 inches deep (0.25 ft):

20 × 4 × 0.25 ÷ 27 = 0.74 cubic yards, or about 20 cubic feet — roughly 10 bags of 2-cubic-foot mulch.

Covering multiple beds? The mulch calculator adds each area together, handles bag counts, and includes a waste buffer — no math required.

How Deep Should the Mulch Be?

Depth is the biggest variable. Too shallow and weeds push through; too deep and water can't reach the roots.

3"
Sweet spot for most garden beds
2"

Use caseRecommended depth
Top-up on existing mulch1–2 inches
Flower beds and annuals2–3 inches
Shrubs and perennials3 inches
Trees (keep away from trunk)3–4 inches

One rule that never changes: keep mulch 2–3 inches away from plant stems and tree trunks. Piling it against wood traps moisture and causes rot — the classic "mulch volcano" mistake.

Which Mulch Is Right for Your Project?

Mulch type affects how long it lasts, what it does for your soil, and how it looks. Here's what to know.

Shredded Hardwood Bark

The most common choice for garden beds. It knits together as it settles, which means it stays put in rain and wind. Breaks down over 1–2 years, adding organic matter to the soil. Works for almost any ornamental bed. Density is roughly 0.4–0.5 cubic yards per ton — light enough that a cubic yard covers 100 sq ft at 3 inches.

Wood Chips

Chunkier than shredded bark, wood chips decompose more slowly (2–4 years) and are often cheaper — sometimes free from tree-trimming companies. Excellent for around trees and in paths. Less tidy-looking than processed bark for formal beds.

Pine Straw (Pine Needles)

Lightweight, easy to spread, and naturally acidic — ideal for acid-loving plants like azaleas, blueberries, and rhododendrons. Doesn't compact, so water gets through easily. Common in the Southeast US. Coverage varies: one bale covers about 35–50 sq ft at 3 inches.

Rubber Mulch

Made from recycled tires. Doesn't decompose, so you're not replacing it every year, and it stays put in heavy rain. Popular for playgrounds. Doesn't improve soil at all and can get very hot in summer sun — avoid it around edible plants.

Straw

The go-to for vegetable gardens. Cheap, easy to work with, and breaks down quickly to feed the soil. Needs refreshing every season. Make sure you're buying straw (stalks), not hay (seeds) — hay introduces weeds.

Cost Estimate

Mulch prices vary by type and whether you buy bulk or bagged. Bulk is almost always cheaper for any project over a few cubic yards.

per cubic yard, bulk hardwood (typical)
$5–8

At $30/cubic yard, covering four 4×10 ft beds at 3 inches deep takes about 1.5 cubic yards — roughly $45 in bulk mulch. The same job in bags (about 20 bags at $6 each) runs $120. Bulk wins on anything over 2 cubic yards.

Many suppliers have a delivery minimum (often 2–3 cubic yards). If your project is smaller, bagged mulch from a hardware store avoids the delivery fee.

Before You Order — Checklist

  • Add 10% for waste. Mulch settles as it dries, and you'll lose some to edges and irregular shapes. A 10% buffer means one delivery covers the whole job.
  • Measure all your beds. For L-shaped or irregular beds, break them into rectangles, calculate each separately, then add. The mulch calculator handles multiple areas.
  • Check existing depth. If there's already 2 inches of mulch, you only need 1 inch to top up to 3 — not a full 3-inch application. Probing with a stick takes 30 seconds.
  • Keep away from trunks and stems. Leave a 2–3 inch gap around every plant. No mulch volcanoes.
  • Confirm bulk vs. bagged. Under ~2 cubic yards, bagged is more convenient. Over 2 cubic yards, bulk delivery almost always saves money.
  • Check color fade. Dyed mulch (black, red) fades in UV over a season. Natural hardwood turns gray. If color matters, factor in a refresh or choose a naturally dark material like cocoa mulch.

Common questions

How many bags of mulch do I need?

Divide your total cubic feet by the bag size. A bed 10 × 4 ft at 3 inches deep = 40 sq ft × 0.25 ft = 10 cubic feet. Ten cubic feet ÷ 2 (bag size) = 5 bags. The mulch calculator does the bag math automatically once you set your bag size.

How much does a cubic yard of mulch cover?

One cubic yard covers roughly 100 sq ft at 3 inches, 160 sq ft at 2 inches, or 80 sq ft at 4 inches. A quick rule of thumb: 100 sq ft per yard at the standard 3-inch depth.

How often do I need to refresh mulch?

Organic mulches (bark, wood chips, straw) break down over time. Most garden beds need topping up once a year — usually in spring. A 1–2 inch top-up restores depth without over-mulching. Check with a stick in autumn to see how much has decomposed.

Is bulk or bagged mulch better?

Bulk is cheaper per cubic yard once you're ordering more than 2–3 yards — but you need to be home for delivery and have somewhere to put the pile. Bags are easier to handle in small quantities, available immediately, and let you mix types. For a single small bed, bags win on convenience. For a full garden refresh, bulk almost always wins on cost.
