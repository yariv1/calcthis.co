How Much Mulch Do I Need?

Mulch is one of the best things you can do for your garden — but ordering the wrong amount means a second trip to the supplier or a pile of leftover bags. Here's how to get the number right the first time.

The Formula

Mulch volume is simple: area times depth, converted to cubic yards (how landscapers sell it in bulk) or cubic feet (how bags are measured).

Length (ft) × Width (ft) × Depth (ft) ÷ 27 = Cubic yards 1 cubic yard = 27 cubic feet · Most bags are 2 cu ft

Example: a garden bed 12 ft long × 4 ft wide at a 3-inch depth (0.25 ft):

12 × 4 × 0.25 ÷ 27 = 0.44 cubic yards, or about 6 bags of 2 cu ft each.

Multiple beds? Add each area, then multiply by depth once. Or let the mulch calculator handle all of it — including bag count, cubic yards, and cost estimate.

How Deep Should Mulch Be?

Depth is the single biggest variable in your order. Too shallow and it won't suppress weeds or retain moisture. Too deep and you risk smothering roots and promoting rot.

One important rule: never pile mulch directly against plant stems or tree trunks. Leave a 2–3 inch gap around the base. Mulch volcanoes — the cone-shaped mounds you often see around trees — cause bark rot and long-term damage.

Which Mulch Type Is Right for You?

The right mulch depends on where it's going and what you're trying to achieve. Here's how the main options compare.

Shredded Wood / Bark Mulch

The most popular choice for garden beds. Shredded hardwood or bark decomposes slowly, adds organic matter to the soil, and stays in place well. Dark brown varieties give a clean, finished look. Best for: flower beds, shrub borders, tree rings.

Wood Chips

Coarser than shredded bark — larger irregular pieces that lock together under foot traffic and don't blow around. Excellent for pathways and play areas. Decomposes more slowly, so it lasts longer before needing a top-up. Best for: paths, around fruit trees, natural-style plantings.

Pine Straw (Pine Needles)

Lightweight, easy to spread, and very effective at moisture retention. Slightly acidic as it breaks down — ideal around acid-loving plants like blueberries, azaleas, and rhododendrons. Best for: sloped beds where heavy mulch would wash, acid-loving plants.

Rubber Mulch

Made from recycled tyres. Doesn't decompose, so it never needs replacing — but it also adds nothing to the soil. Gets very hot in direct sun and doesn't look natural. Best for: play areas and pathways where permanence matters more than soil health.

Straw

Cheap, effective, and biodegrades quickly. The go-to for vegetable gardens and new lawn seeding. Breaks down in one season, adding organic matter — plan to reapply each year. Best for: vegetable beds, seeded areas, winter protection.

Bagged mulch (typically 2 cubic feet per bag) is convenient for small jobs — you can buy exactly what you need and load it yourself. But the per-cubic-yard cost is significantly higher than bulk delivery.

For anything over 2 cubic yards (about 27 bags), a bulk delivery from a landscaping supplier almost always saves money, even after the delivery fee. Get a local quote and compare.

Before You Order — Checklist

  • Measure every bed. Break irregular shapes into rectangles, calculate each, then add the areas. Don't eyeball it — mulch is heavy and returns are a pain.
  • Add 10% for waste. Uneven ground, edges, and spreading loss all eat into your order. A 10% buffer prevents a second trip.
  • Check existing depth before ordering. If you already have 1.5 inches of old mulch, you only need 1–1.5 inches of top-up, not a full 3-inch layer.
  • Keep mulch away from stems and trunks. Leave a 2–3 inch gap at the base of every plant. Mulch touching bark traps moisture and causes rot.
  • Confirm bulk delivery access. Bulk mulch arrives by truck and is dumped on your driveway. Make sure the driver has room and you can move it quickly.
  • Check colour fastness. Dyed mulches (black, red) look sharp but fade in sun. Natural brown and undyed hardwood hold colour better long-term.

Common Questions

How many bags of mulch do I need for a 10×10 bed?

A 10×10 ft bed (100 sq ft) at 2 inches deep needs 0.62 cubic yards, which is about 8–9 bags of 2 cu ft each. At 3 inches deep: 0.93 cubic yards, about 13 bags. Use the mulch calculator to enter your exact dimensions.

How much does a yard of mulch cover?

One cubic yard covers approximately 108 sq ft at 3 inches deep, 162 sq ft at 2 inches, or 324 sq ft at 1 inch. The exact coverage depends on the mulch type — finer shredded bark spreads slightly thinner than coarser wood chips.

How often should I remulch?

Most organic mulches (shredded bark, wood chips) need topping up every 1–2 years. Straw breaks down in a single season. Rubber mulch lasts indefinitely but adds nothing to the soil. Check depth in spring — if it's below 1.5 inches, add a fresh layer to bring it back to 2–3 inches.

Is it OK to put mulch over weeds?

Yes — a 3–4 inch layer of mulch suppresses most weeds by blocking light. For heavy weed pressure, lay a layer of cardboard or landscape fabric first, then mulch on top. Avoid landscape fabric under permanent plantings though — it degrades over time and becomes a tangled mess.

What's the difference between mulch and compost?

Mulch goes on top of the soil to suppress weeds, retain moisture, and regulate temperature — it's not mixed in. Compost is worked into the soil to improve its structure and fertility. You can use both: add compost first, then mulch on top.
