How Much Gravel Do I Need for a Driveway?

Whether you're building a new gravel driveway or topping up an existing one, a quick calculation before you call the supplier saves you from an expensive second delivery — or a yard of wasted stone sitting in your garage.

The Formula

The math is three steps: find your volume in cubic feet, convert to cubic yards, then multiply by density to get tons.

Length (ft) × Width (ft) × Depth (ft) ÷ 27 = Cubic yards Multiply cubic yards by material density (typically 1.4–1.6) to get tons

A standard 2-car driveway runs about 20 ft wide × 40 ft long. At a 4-inch depth (0.333 ft), that's:

20 × 40 × 0.333 ÷ 27 = 9.9 cubic yards, or roughly 15 tons of crushed stone.

How Deep Should the Gravel Be?

Depth is the number that changes most depending on your situation. More depth means more stability — and a bigger order.

For a brand-new driveway on bare ground, most pros recommend two layers: a compacted base of crusher run (4–6 inches) topped with a finish layer of #57 crushed stone (2–3 inches). This costs more upfront but holds up under vehicle weight without rutting. For a simple refresh, 1–2 inches on top of the existing gravel is usually all you need.

Which Gravel Is Right for a Driveway?

Not all gravel performs equally under tire pressure. Here's what to know before you choose.

Crushed Stone (#57 Limestone or Granite)

The go-to choice for driveways. Angular edges interlock under pressure, which means the stones stay put instead of migrating to the edges. A density of about 1.6 tons per cubic yard. Best for: any driveway that sees regular vehicle traffic.

Crusher Run (Processed Gravel)

A mix of angular stone and stone dust that compacts into an almost-solid surface. Ideal for the base layer of a new driveway. Compacts so well that some people use it as the only layer. Density varies 1.4–1.6 tons/yd³.

Pea Gravel

Round, smooth, and visually attractive — but it rolls under tires and drifts to the edges without containment. Better for walkways and decorative areas. If you want the look, use it as a surface layer over a crushed stone base, with solid edging on both sides.

River Rock

Large, smooth, heavy. Beautiful for garden borders but poor for driveways — round stones don't interlock and shift badly under load. Skip it for any area that takes vehicle weight.

Before You Order — Checklist

  • Add 10% for waste. Gravel settles, compacts, and some is lost to uneven ground. Order slightly more than your calculation suggests.
  • Measure all irregular areas. For L-shaped or curved driveways, break the area into rectangles, calculate each, then add them up.
  • Check the subgrade. Soft or clay-heavy ground benefits from a geotextile fabric layer underneath the base — it prevents the gravel from slowly sinking into the soil.
  • Plan your edging. Without a border (metal, timber, or plastic), gravel migrates over time. Install edging before you pour.
  • Confirm delivery access. Most trucks need space to dump or extend a chute. Ask the supplier about your specific driveway length and any overhead obstructions.
  • Compact in layers. If laying more than 3–4 inches, compact each 2-inch layer with a plate compactor before adding the next. Dumping it all at once leads to soft spots.

Common questions

How much gravel for a 2-car driveway?

A typical 2-car driveway (20 × 40 ft) at 4 inches deep needs about 9.9 cubic yards or 15 tons of crushed stone. Bump that to 6 inches (new driveway base layer) and you're looking at about 14.8 cubic yards / 22 tons. Use the gravel calculator to enter your exact dimensions.

How many cubic yards is a ton of gravel?

It depends on the material. Crushed stone runs about 0.63 cubic yards per ton (1.6 tons/yd³). Pea gravel and river rock are slightly lighter — about 0.71 cubic yards per ton (1.4 tons/yd³). Your supplier may give you a more precise figure for the specific stone they carry.

One layer or two?

For a new driveway, two layers is the better long-term investment: 4–6 inches of compacted crusher run as a base, topped with 2–3 inches of #57 crushed stone as the finish surface. This costs more upfront but creates a firm, load-bearing base that won't rut. For a driveway that just needs freshening, a 1–2 inch single top-up layer works fine.

How often do gravel driveways need a refresh?

Most gravel driveways need topping up every 3–5 years. Gravel settles, compacts, and migrates to the edges over time — especially in high-traffic areas and after heavy rain. A 1–2 inch top-up typically restores the surface without full excavation.
