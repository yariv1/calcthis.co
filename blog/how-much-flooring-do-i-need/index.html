How Much Flooring Do I Need?

Buy too little and you're back at the store hoping the dye lot still matches; buy too much and you've paid for boxes you'll never open. The fix is simple — measure the area, add the right waste factor for your material and layout, then round up to whole boxes.

The Formula

Flooring is sold and installed by area, so every estimate starts from the square footage of the room. Once you have that, you inflate it by a waste factor and divide by the coverage printed on each box.

Length (ft) × Width (ft) = Floor area (sq ft) Boxes = Floor area × (1 + waste %) ÷ coverage per box

A 12 × 15 ft bedroom is 180 sq ft. Add a 10% waste factor for hardwood and you need 198 sq ft of material. If each box covers 20 sq ft, that's 9.9 boxes → round up to 10.

Never round the box count down. A part-box short means a second trip, and possibly a visibly different dye lot if the store has restocked.

The Waste Factor Is the Part Everyone Gets Wrong

This is the single biggest reason DIY flooring orders come up short. Every plank you cut to fit against a wall leaves an offcut that's usually too small to reuse — and the more complex your layout, the more you throw away. The waste factor is not padding; it's material that ends up in the bin.

10%
The safe default for a standard straight-lay installation in a simple rectangular room
20%
What a diagonal or herringbone pattern can demand once every plank is cut to an angle
Material / layoutAdd for waste
Vinyl plank / laminate, straight lay5%

Herringbone, chevron, or heavy pattern15–20%
Rooms with many angles, closets, or jogs+5% on top

Two rules of thumb layer on top of the table. First, a bigger room needs a smaller percentage — waste is proportionally lower across a large open floor than a small choppy one. Second, if you're a first-time installer, nudge your percentage up: your early cuts will be your worst, and the extra half-box is cheap insurance against a mid-project stall.

What's Under the Floor Matters Too

A floating floor is only as good as the layers beneath it. When you're pricing a job, don't forget the underlayment — it's a separate purchase measured in the same square footage as the flooring itself.

Most laminate and vinyl plank floors float over a thin foam or cork underlayment that cushions the floor, deadens sound, and — on concrete — blocks moisture. Buy it in the same square footage as your flooring (no waste factor needed; it butts edge to edge). Some premium planks ship with a pad already attached, so check the box before double-buying.

From Square Feet to Boxes

Flooring is bought by the box, not the square foot — and box coverage varies a lot by product, so the printed number on the label is the one that counts, not a rule of thumb.

20 sq ft
Typical coverage of a single box of laminate or vinyl plank
10
Boxes for a 180 sq ft bedroom at 10% waste and 20 sq ft/box

Always divide your waste-adjusted area by the box coverage, then round up to the next whole box. If the result lands just over a whole number — say 10.1 boxes — buy 11; you don't want a project stopped cold by a single missing plank. Keep at least one unopened box aside as an attic stash for future repairs; discontinued lines are impossible to match years later.

A 320 sq ft living room in mid-range vinyl plank at $3/sq ft, with 10% waste, works out to about $1,056 in material (352 sq ft × $3). The same room in solid hardwood at $6/sq ft is roughly $2,112. Professional installation typically adds $2–$6 per square foot on top, depending on material, subfloor prep, and how complex the layout is.

Before You Buy — Checklist

  • Measure twice, at the widest points. Include closets, alcoves, and doorway thresholds. For irregular rooms, break the space into rectangles and add the areas together.
  • Pick your waste factor before you order. 5% for straight-lay vinyl/laminate, 10% for hardwood, 15–20% for diagonal or herringbone. When unsure, round up.
  • Match the dye lot. Buy all your flooring in one order so every box comes from the same production run — colour and grain can shift noticeably between lots.
  • Don't forget underlayment. Same square footage as the floor, bought separately — unless your planks have a pad pre-attached.
  • Acclimate the boxes. Most wood and laminate needs 48–72 hours flat in the room before installation so the planks adjust to the home's humidity and don't gap or buckle later.
  • Leave an expansion gap. Floating floors need a small gap (usually about 3/8 inch) around every wall so the floor can move with temperature and humidity. Baseboards or quarter-round hide it.
  • Keep a spare box. Store one unopened box for repairs — matching a discontinued floor later is nearly impossible.

Common Questions

How much extra flooring should I buy for waste?

Add 5% for straight-lay vinyl plank or laminate, 10% for hardwood, and 15–20% for diagonal, herringbone, or chevron patterns. Small, choppy rooms and first-time installers should lean toward the higher end — the offcuts from cutting planks to fit are usually too short to reuse, so that percentage really does get thrown away.

How do I calculate flooring for an irregular or L-shaped room?

Split the floor into simple rectangles, calculate the area of each (length × width), and add them together. Apply your waste factor once to the combined total, not to each rectangle separately. For rooms with lots of jogs, closets, or angles, add an extra 5% on top of your normal waste factor to cover the additional cuts.

How many boxes of flooring do I need?

Take your floor area, multiply by (1 + your waste factor), then divide by the coverage printed on the box — and always round up to the next whole box. For a 180 sq ft room at 10% waste with 20 sq ft boxes: 180 × 1.10 ÷ 20 = 9.9, so buy 10 boxes. Box coverage varies by product, so use the actual label figure rather than assuming.

Do I need underlayment under my flooring?

Usually yes, for floating floors like laminate and vinyl plank — it cushions the floor, reduces noise, and acts as a moisture barrier over concrete. Buy it in the same square footage as your flooring; it lays edge to edge with no waste factor. The exception is planks that come with a pad already attached, and glue-down or nail-down installations, which follow different rules — check your product's instructions.
