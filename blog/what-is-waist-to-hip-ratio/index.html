What Is Waist-to-Hip Ratio? How to Measure and Interpret Your WHR

Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) compares how much you carry around your middle to how much you carry on your hips — and it turns out where your weight sits matters almost as much as how much of it there is. Here's exactly how to measure it, what the number means, and how it stacks up against BMI.

What waist-to-hip ratio actually measures

Waist-to-hip ratio is exactly what it sounds like: your waist circumference divided by your hip circumference. It's not a measure of how much you weigh — two people at the identical weight and height can have very different WHRs, because the number captures fat distribution, not total body size.

That distinction matters because abdominal fat behaves differently than fat stored on the hips and thighs. Fat around the waist sits closer to the liver, pancreas, and other organs, and is more strongly linked to metabolic problems — type 2 diabetes, heart disease, high blood pressure — than the same amount of fat carried lower down. WHR is a simple, at-home way to flag that pattern before it shows up on a lab report.

How to measure your waist and hips correctly

The two measurements are quick, but getting them right matters more than most people expect — a tape measure pulled too tight or placed in the wrong spot can shift your ratio into a different risk category entirely.

  • Waist: find the narrowest point of your torso, roughly midway between the bottom of your ribs and the top of your hip bones — for most people this lands close to belly-button level. Keep the tape snug but not compressing the skin, and take the reading after breathing out normally, not while holding your stomach in.
  • Hips: measure around the widest part of your buttocks, with the tape held level all the way around — check in a mirror or ask someone to confirm it isn't riding up in the back.
  • Take each twice. Measure both waist and hip twice and average the two readings — a single measurement is easy to get slightly wrong, and averaging smooths that out.

The formula

WHR = Waist ÷ Hip UNITLESS — WORKS THE SAME IN INCHES OR CENTIMETERS, AS LONG AS BOTH SIDES MATCH

Because it's a ratio of two lengths, the units cancel out — a WHR of 0.83 means the same thing whether you measured in inches or centimeters, as long as you used the same unit for both waist and hip.

Worked example

A woman with a 34 in waist and 41 in hips: 34 ÷ 41 ≈ 0.83. Checked against the WHO female bands below, that lands in the moderate-risk range (0.81–0.85) — not a cause for alarm on its own, but worth tracking alongside other measures.

Apple vs. pear (and everything in between)

You'll see these informal labels everywhere WHR is discussed. A lower ratio — more weight on the hips and thighs — is often called pear-shaped. A higher ratio — more weight around the middle — is often called apple-shaped. People in the moderate band are sometimes described as avocado-shaped, a newer, gentler label for "somewhere in between." None of these are medical categories; they're just a shorthand for where the number falls, and apple-shaped fat distribution is the one linked to higher metabolic risk.

Why waist-to-hip ratio matters more than the scale alone

Some research has found WHR to be a stronger predictor of cardiovascular risk and mortality than BMI alone — particularly in older adults — precisely because it captures where fat sits rather than just how much of it exists. Two people with an identical BMI can have meaningfully different health risk if one carries their extra weight around the middle and the other carries it on the hips.

WHR vs. BMI vs. waist circumference alone

Each of the three common measurements tells you something different, and none replaces the others:

  • BMI estimates overall weight relative to height. It's a useful population-level screening number, but it can't distinguish muscle from fat and says nothing about where fat sits.
  • Waist circumference alone is a simple, direct read on abdominal size. The World Health Organization flags increased risk above roughly 35 in for women and 40 in for men — an independent signal even without a hip measurement.
  • Waist-to-hip ratio adds context by comparing that waist size to your body's own frame, showing fat distribution rather than raw size — useful for comparing risk between two people of different builds.
  • Waist-to-height ratio is a fourth, less common option: keeping your waist under half your height (for someone 5 ft 6 in (66 in) tall, that means staying under roughly 33 in) is a widely cited general guideline that some clinicians use as a quick cross-check.

Doctors typically use two or three of these together, not just one — that's exactly what the waist-to-hip ratio calculator's advanced mode does, cross-checking your ratio against the absolute waist-circumference threshold and, if you add your height, the waist-to-height guideline too.

Can you change your waist-to-hip ratio?

Yes. WHR responds to the same things that reduce abdominal fat generally: a modest, sustainable calorie deficit, regular aerobic exercise, strength training (which helps preserve muscle while fat comes off), enough sleep, and managing chronic stress, which raises cortisol and is linked to abdominal fat storage specifically. There's no way to "spot reduce" fat from just the waist — but overall fat loss tends to shrink waist circumference proportionally more than hip circumference for most people, which is why WHR usually improves as total body fat drops.

Common questions

What is a healthy waist-to-hip ratio?

For women, 0.80 or below is generally considered low risk, 0.81–0.85 moderate, and 0.86 or above higher risk. For men the cutoffs are higher: 0.95 or below is low risk, 0.96–1.00 moderate, and above 1.00 higher risk. These bands come from World Health Organization guidance and are a commonly used framework, not a diagnosis.

How do I measure my waist and hips correctly?

Measure your waist at the midpoint between the bottom of your ribs and the top of your hip bone, using a snug but not tight tape, after breathing out normally. Measure your hips at the widest part of your buttocks, with the tape level all the way around. Take each measurement twice and average them.

Is waist-to-hip ratio better than BMI?

They measure different things. BMI estimates overall weight relative to height but says nothing about where fat is stored. WHR shows fat distribution, and abdominal fat carries a higher health risk than fat stored on the hips and thighs at the same BMI. Doctors typically use both together, plus waist circumference alone, for a fuller picture.

What does an apple vs. pear body shape mean?

These are informal, non-medical labels for where your ratio falls. A lower WHR (more weight on the hips and thighs) is often called "pear-shaped." A higher WHR (more weight around the waist) is often called "apple-shaped." Apple-shaped fat distribution is linked to a higher risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes because it sits closer to internal organs.

Can I improve my waist-to-hip ratio?

Yes — WHR responds to the same things that reduce abdominal fat generally: a modest calorie deficit, regular aerobic and strength exercise, adequate sleep, and stress management. There's no way to "spot reduce" fat from just the waist, but overall fat loss tends to reduce waist circumference proportionally more than hip circumference for most people.
