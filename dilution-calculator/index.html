    How to use the dilution calculator

    Choose how the dilution is given. Strengths: type the strength you have and the strength you need, each with its unit. Ratio: type it the way you read it, 1:10, 1 in 10 or 10x; a plain number is read as the choice on the right. Volumes: type the concentrate and the water you already mixed to see the ratio and the strength you made.

    Then say which volume you know: the final volume you want to make, the concentrate you have, or the water you have. You get the concentrate and the water to mix, the flask to scale, the mixing order and a table of the same batch at other strengths. Under Go advanced you can make extra to cover what sticks to the container.

    The dilution formula

    C1 × V1 = C2 × V2 ADDING WATER CHANGES THE VOLUME, NOT THE AMOUNT OF SOLUTE
    You wantFormula
    Concentrate to useV1 = C2 × V2 ÷ C1
    Water to addV2 − V1
    Dilution factorC1 ÷ C2 = V2 ÷ V1
    Ratio 1 : XX = factor − 1

    C1 and V1 are the strength and volume of the concentrate, C2 and V2 the strength and volume you end up with. Use the same unit for C1 and C2 and the same unit for the volumes; this calculator converts units for you, but never between mass, molar and × units.

    1:10, 1 in 10 and 10× are not the same

    Written asPartsFactor
    1 : 31 part concentrate + 3 parts water = 4 parts4× weaker
    1 in 41 part concentrate in 4 parts, so 3 parts water4× weaker
    4×1 part concentrate + 3 parts water4× weaker
    1 : 101 part concentrate + 10 parts water = 11 parts11× weaker

    Always check which one a recipe or label means. This calculator writes out what it read: “1 : 10 means 1 part concentrate + 10 parts water = 11 parts”.

    Worked examples

    10% concentrate to 1%, making 1 L: the factor is 10, so 100 mL of concentrate and 900 mL of water.

    Lab stock, 10 mM to 100 µM, making 50 mL: the factor is 100, so 0.5 mL of stock and 49.5 mL of diluent.

    Ratio 1 : 10, making 500 mL: 11 parts in all, so 45.45 mL of concentrate and 454.5 mL of water.

    2.0 M stock to 0.40 M, making 500 mL: 100 mL of stock and 400 mL of water (the LibreTexts example).

    Limits and safety

    The calculator assumes the liquids simply add up and the solute does not react, so it suits water-based mixes, cleaners, stock solutions and brines. A percent by weight with different densities, alcohol-water mixes and solutes that react need more than C1V1. Add the concentrate to the water, never the other way round for acids and other strong concentrates, and never mix bleach with other cleaners.

    For doses of a liquid medicine from the strength on its label, use the mg to mL calculator; for a peptide vial, the peptide reconstitution calculator.

    Sources

    • The rule behind the calculator: LibreTexts, Chemistry, 13.7 Solution Dilution (last modified 18 February 2026, read 2026-11-21): adding water changes the volume and the concentration but the number of moles of solute does not change, so M1 V1 = M2 V2. Its worked example (100 mL of 2.0 M HCl made up to 500 mL gives 0.40 M) is reproduced above.
    • ppm and mg/L: Yale, Water Knowledge for All, Units for Pollutant Concentrations (read 2026-11-21): since 1 L of fresh water has a mass of 1 kg, 1 mg/L is 1 mg/kg, also called 1 part per million. This holds for dilute water-like liquids only. In this calculator % means grams per 100 mL, so 1 % = 10 g/L = 10,000 mg/L = 10,000 ppm.
    • Mixing order: University of Illinois Division of Research Safety, Mineral Acids (read 2026-11-21): always dilute by adding acid to water and not water to acid; adding water to concentrated acid may cause violent boiling and splashing. The CDC page Cleaning and Disinfecting with Bleach (read 2026-11-21): never mix household bleach with any other cleaners or disinfectants, and bleach solutions will not be as effective after being mixed with water for over 24 hours.
    • Volume units: NIST Special Publication 811, Appendix B.8 (read 2026-11-12 and 2026-11-21): US cup 236.5882 mL, US fluid ounce 29.57353 mL, US teaspoon 4.928922 mL, US tablespoon 14.78676 mL, US gallon 3.785412 L, imperial gallon 4.54609 L. The UK fluid ounce (4.54609 L ÷ 160), pint (÷ 8) are derived from the imperial gallon; the 160 and 8 are not on the NIST page.
    • Not handled: percent by weight (w/w) with different densities, solutes that react, precipitate or evaporate, and liquids that do not mix ideally (ethanol and water shrink a little when mixed). Ask the supplier or the safety data sheet for those.

    For information only. Strong acids, bases, bleach and other chemicals can burn or give off harmful fumes: follow the label and the safety data sheet, wear goggles and gloves, and never mix cleaners.

    Common questions

    How do you calculate a dilution?

    Use C1 × V1 = C2 × V2: the amount of solute stays the same when you add water. The concentrate you need is V1 = C2 × V2 ÷ C1, and the water is V2 − V1. To make 500 mL at 0.40 M from a 2.0 M stock you need 100 mL of stock and 400 mL of water.

    What does a 1:10 dilution mean?

    1:10 means 1 part concentrate and 10 parts water, 11 parts in all, so the strength is 9.091% of the concentrate. “1 in 10” or “10×” means 1 part in 10 parts in all, so 9 parts water. The calculator reads both and writes out which one you typed.

    How much concentrate do I need for 1 litre at 1:10?

    For 1 L at a 1:10 ratio: 90.91 mL of concentrate and 909.1 mL of water. For 1 in 10 (10×) it is 100 mL of concentrate and 900 mL of water.

    How do I dilute 10% to 1%?

    The factor is 10 ÷ 1 = 10. For 1 L you need 100 mL of the 10% concentrate and 900 mL of water. For other sizes scale both amounts.

    How do I dilute a stock in mM to µM?

    Type the stock in mM and the target in µM; the calculator converts the units. Diluting 10 mM to 100 µM is a factor of 100, so for 50 mL you need 0.5 mL of stock and 49.5 mL of diluent.

    Can I dilute to a stronger solution?

    No. Adding water can only make a liquid weaker, so the target must be below the strength you have. The calculator says so if you type a stronger target.

    Does this work for alcohol or percent by weight?

    It works when the amounts of concentrate and water simply add up. Alcohol and water shrink a little when mixed, and a percent by weight needs the densities. For example, 2 L at 70% from 99% needs about 1,414 mL of the 99% and 585.9 mL of water if the volumes add up exactly; check the label or the safety data sheet for the real figure.

    Which goes in first, the water or the concentrate?

    Add the concentrate to the water, slowly and stirring. University safety guidance for acids is to always add acid to water and not water to acid, because water added to a concentrate can boil violently and splash. Never mix bleach with other cleaners, and use a bleach solution within 24 hours (CDC).

    How do I convert ppm to percent?

    For water-like liquids 1 ppm is 1 mg/L, and 1 % (g per 100 mL) is 10 g/L, so 1 % = 10,000 ppm and 100 ppm = 0.01 %. You can type the stock in one unit and the target in another of the same kind and the calculator converts.
