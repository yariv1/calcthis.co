What BSA is used for

Body surface area scales some drug doses, indexes kidney function to body size (eGFR is reported per 1.73 m²) and describes burns. Because it comes from height and weight alone, it does not tell fat from muscle. For other body measures see the BMI and body fat calculators.

Common questions

What is body surface area (BSA)?

Body surface area is the total area of the skin, in square metres, estimated from height and weight. It is used in medicine to scale some drug doses, to index measurements such as kidney function to body size, and in burn care to describe the burned fraction (Redlarski 2016 lists burns, liver-volume indexing and drug dosing among its uses). It says nothing about how much of your weight is fat or muscle.

How do I calculate BSA?

Mosteller: BSA (m²) = √(height in cm × weight in kg ÷ 3,600). For 170 cm and 60 kg: 170 × 60 = 10,200; ÷ 3,600 = 2.83; square root = 1.68 m². Du Bois: 0.007184 × weight^0.425 × height^0.725 gives 1.69 m² for the same person. The calculator does it for eight formulas at once, in kg or lb or st and cm or inches or metres.

Which BSA formula is the most accurate?

There is no single best one — the formulas were fitted on different groups of people and disagree. For the woman of 165 cm and 60 kg they range from 1.59 to 1.67 m² (5.1% apart), for the man of 180 cm and 80 kg from 1.92 to 2.01 m² (5.0%), and for a 120 cm, 22 kg child from 0.71 to 0.87 m² (22.4%). A 2016 comparison of 25 formulae in Scientific Reports called the ambiguity alarming. Use the formula your clinic, study or protocol specifies.

Which formula is used for children and infants?

Haycock (1978) was fitted on 81 subjects from premature infants to adults, so it is the usual choice across that whole range. The calculator accepts weights from 0.5 kg and heights from 30 cm, shows every formula, and lets you make Haycock the headline by clicking its row. For babies and small children the formulas can differ a lot — check which one your protocol names.

What is a normal BSA?

There is no “normal” to aim for; BSA just describes body size. The figure 1.73 m² is the reference body surface area used to index kidney function: eGFR is reported in mL/min per 1.73 m². The scale on the calculator marks 1.73 m² so you can see where you sit against it.

How do I convert eGFR to mL/min with my BSA?

Under Go advanced enter the eGFR your lab reported (mL/min/1.73 m²). The calculator multiplies it by your BSA and divides by 1.73 — de-indexing it to mL/min, as in Australian Prescriber. For an eGFR of 60 and a BSA of 1.66 m² that is 58 mL/min. The result depends on the BSA formula you pick; clinical decisions belong with your doctor or pharmacist.

How do I calculate a dose from BSA?

Multiply the dose per m² by the BSA: 100 mg/m² for a BSA of 1.66 m² is 166 mg. Under Go advanced the calculator also shows the dose across all eight formulas, because the choice of formula can change the dose by up to 5.1% for the same person. It uses the unrounded BSA. Your protocol sets the formula, the rounding and any cap, so use it only to check arithmetic.

Should chemotherapy be dosed by actual weight in obesity?

ASCO’s 2012 guideline (Griggs et al.) says doses of cytotoxic chemotherapy for obese adults should be based on actual body weight, with no cap or adjusted weight, for most drugs; a few (vincristine, bleomycin, carboplatin) are dosed differently. That is guidance for clinicians, not something this calculator applies — it does not cap or adjust the BSA.

Is BSA the same as BMI?

No. BMI is weight divided by height squared, a screening number for weight relative to height. BSA estimates the area of the skin and is used for dosing and for indexing physiological measurements. Both use only height and weight — see the BMI calculator.
