Riegel vs VDOT

The Riegel formula, T2 = T1 × (D2/D1)1.06, is quick and works well between roughly half and double your input distance. It tends to be optimistic for big jumps — a marathon predicted from a 5K, for example.

The VDOT model from Jack Daniels and Jimmy Gilbert estimates the oxygen cost of your pace and how long you can hold a share of your maximum, which usually makes it steadier across large distance gaps. Where the two disagree, the truth is often in between.

Getting a reliable prediction

Predictions assume you've trained for the target distance. A short race says what you're capable of aerobically, but a marathon still demands long-run endurance the formula can't see. Use the number as a target to train toward, not a guarantee.

What VDOT means

VDOT is a single number that captures your current running fitness from a race result. A higher VDOT means a faster runner. It's handy for comparing efforts across distances: a 20:00 5K and a 41:30 10K are almost the same VDOT, so either can seed your predictions.

Common questions

How does a race time predictor work?

It takes the distance and time of one recent race and estimates your finish time at other distances. This calculator runs two models at once — Riegel, a simple endurance formula, and VDOT, the Daniels & Gilbert oxygen-cost model — so you see a realistic range rather than a single guess.

What is the Riegel formula?

Peter Riegel's formula predicts a new time as T2 = T1 × (D2/D1)1.06. The 1.06 exponent captures how pace slows as distance grows. It's accurate roughly between half and double your input distance and tends to be optimistic for very long extrapolations.

What is VDOT?

VDOT is a running fitness score from Jack Daniels and Jimmy Gilbert. It estimates the oxygen cost of your race pace and how long you can hold a percentage of your maximum, then converts that into equivalent times at other distances. It usually handles big distance jumps better than Riegel.

Which prediction should I trust?

Predictions are most reliable when the target is close to the race you entered and you've built the endurance for it. For a marathon predicted from a short race, treat the number as a ceiling that assumes proper long-run training. When in doubt, aim between the two models.
