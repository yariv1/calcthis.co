    How to use the serial dilution calculator

    Type the stock concentration and choose its unit. Then choose how you set the series: Factor and tubes (a factor such as 10 and a number of tubes), From X down to Y (the first and last concentration, and the page finds the factor or the number of tubes), or My own list (the concentration of every tube).

    Add the liquid each tube must give and the page works out how much to make, take and dilute in every tube, starting from the last. Under Go advanced you can add replicates, an allowance for pipetting loss, the smallest volume you can pipette, the error of one step, and a plate count to find the original concentration.

    The formulas

    tube k = first tube ÷ factor^(k − 1) TUBE 1 = STOCK ÷ FACTOR · TAKE = MADE ÷ FACTOR · DILUENT = MADE − TAKE · ORIGINAL = COUNT ÷ VOLUME PLATED (mL) ÷ TUBE DILUTION

    Worked out with this calculator: a stock of 1,000,000 diluted 10-fold in 6 tubes gives 100,000, 10,000, 1,000, 100, 10, 1. To end with 100 µL in every tube, working backwards:

    TubeConcentrationPipette: take + diluent = make
    1100,00011.11 µL of stock + 100 µL diluent = 111.1 µL
    210,00011.11 µL of tube 1 + 100 µL diluent = 111.1 µL
    31,00011.11 µL of tube 2 + 99.99 µL diluent = 111.1 µL
    410011.1 µL of tube 3 + 99.9 µL diluent = 111 µL
    51011 µL of tube 4 + 99 µL diluent = 110 µL
    6110 µL of tube 5 + 90 µL diluent = 100 µL

    The whole series needs 11.11 µL of stock and 588.9 µL of diluent.

    A half-log series uses a factor of √10 = 3.162: 316,228, 100,000, 31,623, 10,000 for four tubes, so every second tube is a tenfold step.

    From X down to Y

    Choose “From X down to Y” when you know the concentrations you want. From 1,000 down to 1 with a factor of 10 takes 4 tubes; with 4 tubes the factor is 10. If the factor does not divide the range evenly, the last step is smaller so the last tube lands exactly on the concentration you typed.

    Counting back from a plate

    Plate a known volume from one tube and count the colonies. Divide the count by the volume in millilitres, then by the dilution of that tube. In the lab manual example, 32 colonies from 200 µL of a 10−6 tube is 32 ÷ 0.2 = 160 per mL, and 160 ÷ 10−6 = 160,000,000 per mL in the original. A count of 30 to 300 is the range the manual calls countable.

    Error and the smallest volume

    Every step repeats the error of the one before it. With 2% per step, 6 steps give about ±4.899% for independent errors and at most ±12%. If a transfer is smaller than the smallest volume you can pipette, the page flags it: use a smaller factor, more liquid in the tube, or an extra step.

    What the calculator does not do

    It does not choose a factor, a volume or a pipette for you, it does not convert between a mass and a molar concentration, and it does not replace your protocol. It does the arithmetic with the numbers you type.

    Common questions

    How do I calculate a serial dilution?

    Divide the stock concentration by the dilution factor to get tube 1, then divide each tube by the factor again to get the next. For a stock of 1,000,000 and a factor of 10, the tubes are 100,000, 10,000, 1,000, 100, 10, 1. The last tube is 1,000,000 times weaker than the stock.

    What does a 1:10 serial dilution mean?

    A ratio of 1 : 10 means 1 part of the sample and 10 parts of diluent, so 11 parts in total and a factor of 11. A tenfold dilution, often called a 1 in 10 dilution, is 1 part sample and 9 parts diluent, a factor of 10. The page has a switch for “Fold”, “Ratio 1 : X” and “Transfer / total”, so you can type it the way your protocol says it.

    How much do I take and how much diluent do I add?

    The volume to take is the volume you make divided by the step factor, and the diluent is what is left. To end with 100 µL in a tenfold series, tube 6 takes 10 µL and 90 µL of diluent. Earlier tubes must hold more, because the next tube takes some of their liquid.

    Why do the first tubes need more volume?

    Each tube must give 100 µL for your assay and also feed the next tube. Working backwards from the last tube, tube 5 makes 110 µL and tube 1 makes 111.1 µL. The stock needed for the whole series is 11.11 µL.

    How do I get from a concentration to another in the fewest tubes?

    Choose “From X down to Y”, type both concentrations and either the factor or the number of tubes. With 1,000 to 1 and a factor of 10 you need 4 tubes; with 4 tubes the factor is 10.

    How do I find the concentration of the original sample from a plate count?

    Divide the colonies counted by the volume plated in millilitres, then divide by the concentration of the tube it came from (written as a fraction of the original). In the lab manual example, 32 colonies from 200 µL of a 10−6 tube is 160 per mL, so the original has 160,000,000 per mL. Plates with 30 to 300 colonies are called countable.

    Does pipetting error add up along the series?

    Yes. Every step repeats the error of the one before it. If you type 2% for one step, 6 steps give about ±4.899% if the errors are independent and at most ±12% if they all point the same way. The number is yours: no pipette accuracy is assumed.

    Can I use my own list of concentrations?

    Yes. Choose “My own list” and type the concentration of every tube from the strongest to the weakest. The page gives the step between tubes, the volumes to take and diluent to add, and flags any tube that is not weaker than the one before.

    What concentration units does it use?

    Choose M, mM, µM, nM, pM, fM, mg/mL, µg/mL, ng/mL, %, cells/mL, CFU/mL, PFU/mL or ×. The unit is a label only: every tube uses the unit of your stock and nothing is converted.
