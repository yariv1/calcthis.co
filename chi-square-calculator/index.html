      How to use the chi square calculator

      Pick Two-way table to ask whether two things are related, or Goodness of fit to ask whether one list of counts matches what you expect. Type a count in each box (add rows and columns as needed), paste a table from a spreadsheet, or paste one line of raw answers per person and the table builds itself. The result updates as you type.

      Under Go advanced you can name the variables, choose the headline test for a 2×2 table, allow for parameters estimated from the data, plan for an effect size and set the decimal places.

      The chi square formula

      χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E O = observed count · E = expected count · TABLE: E = row total × column total ÷ grand total, df = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1) · LIST: E = total × expected share, df = categories − 1

      Worked example: two groups, three answers

      BoxObservedExpected(O − E)² ÷ E
      A low20251.0000
      A medium30350.7143
      A high50402.5000
      B low30251.0000
      B medium40350.7143
      B high30402.5000

      The contributions add up to χ² = 8.4286 with 2 degrees of freedom. The p-value is 0.01478 and the critical value at 0.05 is 5.9915. Cramér’s V is 0.2053. The two outer columns carry almost all of the difference; the middle one adds 1.4286.

      Goodness of fit examples

      A die rolled 60 times shows 8, 12, 9, 14, 11 and 6. Each face is expected 10 times, so χ² = 4.2000 with 5 degrees of freedom and p = 0.521: nothing unusual for a fair die.

      Counts of 315, 108, 101 and 32 tested against a 9:3:3:1 ratio give χ² = 0.4700 with 3 degrees of freedom and p = 0.9254, a very close fit. A coin showing 62 heads in 100 tosses gives χ² = 5.7600 and p = 0.0164; the exact binomial test gives 0.02098.

      Where the difference comes from

      One χ² number does not say which counts are unusual. The picture on this page does: each row of the table becomes a bar split into its columns, with dashed lines showing where the pieces would end if nothing differed. Amber pieces hold more counts than expected and blue pieces fewer, with stronger colour further from expected. The cell table gives each box its contribution and its standardized difference; beyond ±2 is unusual.

      Small counts: Fisher, Yates and merging

      The chi-square p-value is an approximation. A common rule asks for expected counts of at least 5 in every box of a 2×2 table, and in at least 80% of the boxes of a bigger one with none below 1. For the 2×2 table 20, 10 and 30, 40 the three p-values are 0.0291 (Pearson), 0.04953 (Yates) and 0.04858 (Fisher, two-sided). For 20, 30 and 30, 40 they are 0.7543, 0.9004 and 0.8515.

      In a bigger table you can merge two rows, columns or categories on the page and undo it. Merge only groups that mean something together, and choose them before looking at which choice gives the p-value you like.

      The same people twice: McNemar

      If 314 patients are checked before and after a treatment and 101 are positive both times, 121 go from positive to negative, 59 from negative to positive and 33 are negative both times, only the 180 who changed are tested: χ² = (121 − 59)² ÷ 180 = 21.3556, p = 3.815 × 10⁻⁶. With fewer than 25 changes the exact or mid-p value is the better choice.

      Effect size and sample size

      Cohen’s w = √(χ² ÷ N) measures the size of a difference independently of the number of counts. In the worked example w = 0.2053; if the true shares were exactly those, a new study of 200 counts would reach p below 0.05 74.1% of the time and 229 counts would give 80% power. With 100 counts and 1 degree of freedom, a medium effect (w = 0.3) has 85.1% power.

      Common questions

      What is a chi-square test?

      It compares counts you observed with the counts you would expect if there were nothing going on. The statistic χ² adds up (observed − expected)² ÷ expected over every box. The larger it is for its degrees of freedom, the smaller the p-value.

      What is the difference between independence and goodness of fit?

      Independence takes a two-way table, such as group by answer, and asks whether the two are related. Goodness of fit takes one list of counts, such as the faces of a die, and asks whether they match shares you state in advance.

      How do I calculate expected counts?

      In a table, expected = row total × column total ÷ grand total. In the example the first box is 100 × 50 ÷ 200 = 25. In a goodness-of-fit test, expected = total × expected share.

      How many degrees of freedom are there?

      For a table it is (rows − 1) × (columns − 1), so a 2×3 table has 2. For goodness of fit it is the number of categories − 1, less one for each parameter you estimated from the same data.

      What p-value is significant?

      The usual line is 0.05: a p-value below it counts as significant. You can type another level. The example has p = 0.01478, below 0.05, so the two rows split differently by more than chance usually produces.

      Can I use percentages instead of counts?

      No. The test depends on how many people or items there are: the same percentages from 20 and from 2,000 observations give very different results. Convert percentages back to counts first. Percentages are fine as expected shares in a goodness-of-fit test.

      What if an expected count is below 5?

      The chi-square p-value becomes unreliable when expected counts are small. For a 2×2 table use Fisher’s exact test, which this page shows next to it; for two categories use the exact binomial test; for bigger tables, merge groups that belong together or collect more data.

      When should I use Fisher’s exact test or Yates’s correction?

      Both apply to 2×2 tables. Fisher’s test is exact and suits small counts. Yates’s correction lowers χ² to allow for counts being whole numbers and is more cautious. For the table 20, 10 and 30, 40 the p-values are 0.0291 (Pearson), 0.04953 (Yates) and 0.04858 (Fisher, two-sided).

      When do I need McNemar’s test?

      Use it when the same people were measured twice with a yes or no answer, such as before and after. Only the people who changed enter the test: χ² = (b − c)² ÷ (b + c). Fill a 2×2 table and choose “Same people twice”.

      Does a large χ² mean a strong effect?

      No. χ² grows with the number of counts. Cramér’s V and Cohen’s w describe the size of the effect. In the example w = 0.2053, a small effect by Cohen’s guide.

      How many observations do I need?

      That depends on the effect you want to be able to detect. For 80% power at α = 0.05 with 1 degree of freedom you need about 785 counts for a small effect (w = 0.1), 88 for a medium one (0.3) and 32 for a large one (0.5).

      I only have the χ² value. Can I get the p-value?

      Yes. Choose “χ² value”, type the statistic and the degrees of freedom, and the p-value, the critical value and the curve appear. For example χ² = 3.84 with 1 degree of freedom gives p = 0.05004. With only the degrees of freedom typed, the critical value is shown.
