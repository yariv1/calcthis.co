How to use the loan calculator

Enter the loan amount, the interest rate and the loan term — in years, or switch the box to months — or tap 1, 3, 5, 7 or 10 years. The result updates as you type: your payment, a chart of how the balance falls, how much of the loan is principal and how much is interest, and a full amortization schedule. Type an amount in Extra payment each time to see how much interest it saves and how much sooner the loan ends.

Not sure what to ask for? Switch to What can I borrow?, enter the payment that fits your budget, and the calculator works backwards to the loan that payment carries.

The loan payment formula

M = P · r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n) P = LOAN AMOUNT · r = RATE PER PAYMENT · n = NUMBER OF PAYMENTS

For a $15,000 loan at 8.5% over 5 years: r = 8.5% ÷ 12 = 0.0070833 and n = 60, so (1 + r)60 = 1.5273. Then M = $15,000 × 0.0070833 ÷ (1 − 1 ÷ 1.5273) = $307.75 a month. Over 60 payments you pay $3,464.88 in interest on top of the $15,000 you borrowed.

Why early payments are mostly interest

Interest is charged on what you still owe, and you owe the most at the start. On that loan the first payment is $106.25 in interest and $201.50 in principal. Each payment shrinks the balance a little, so the interest share falls and the principal share rises. The calculator shows each year’s split in the strip under your balance chart.

What extra payments do

Extra money goes straight to principal, so the interest it would have earned the lender is never charged. Adding $50 a month to the loan above ends it after 50 payments — 10 months sooner — and saves $604 in interest.

The rate is not the cost: fees and APR

Many loans charge an origination fee, a percentage of the loan paid to the lender. Under Go advanced you can enter it as a percentage or a dollar amount and say how it is paid: taken out of the loan, added to the loan, or paid upfront. On the $15,000 loan, a 2% fee taken out of the loan is $300 — you receive $14,700 but repay as if you had borrowed $15,000. The calculator then shows the true APR, 9.36% in this case, against the 8.5% rate.

How much can I borrow?

Start from your budget instead of the loan. A payment of $300 a month at 8.5% carries $14,622 over 5 years, $18,944 over 7 years and $24,196 over 10 years. A longer term lets the same payment borrow more, but you pay interest for longer, which is why the comparison table lists the total interest for every term.

Shorter or longer?

A shorter loan costs more each month but far less in total. On $15,000 at 8.5%, 3 years is $473.51 a month with $2,046 of interest; 7 years is $237.55 a month with $4,954. The table in every result shows 1, 3, 5, 7 and 10 years for your own numbers.

Payment frequency and compounding

Go advanced lets you pay daily, weekly, every two weeks, twice a month, monthly, quarterly, twice a year or yearly, and choose how often interest compounds — yearly, twice a year, quarterly, monthly, twice a month, every two weeks, weekly, daily or continuously. Most US loans compound monthly and are paid monthly, which is the default. Switching the $15,000 loan to daily compounding makes the monthly payment $307.96; at 0% interest the payment is just $15,000 ÷ 60 = $250.00. You can also set the start month and year for the payoff date and the schedule.

Common questions

How is a loan payment calculated?

The payment is M = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)⁻ⁿ), where P is the loan amount, r is the interest rate for one payment period (the yearly rate ÷ 12 for monthly payments) and n is the number of payments. For $15,000 at 8.5% over 5 years, r = 0.0070833, n = 60 and the payment is $307.75 a month. At a 0% rate the payment is simply the amount divided by the number of payments: $15,000 ÷ 60 = $250.00.

What is the difference between the interest rate and the APR?

The interest rate is what the lender charges on the balance. The APR also counts fees, so it shows the real cost. On the $15,000 loan at 8.5% with a 2% origination fee taken out of the loan, you pay $307.75 a month but receive only $14,700. Measured against the money you actually receive, the loan costs 9.36% APR. Add the fee under Go advanced and the calculator shows the true APR automatically.

How much do extra payments save on a loan?

On the same $15,000 loan, adding $50 to every monthly payment ends the loan after 50 payments instead of 60 — 10 months sooner — and saves $604 in interest. Every extra dollar goes straight to principal, so it stops accruing interest for the rest of the loan. Type an amount in the extra payment box to see your own savings and the faster balance curve.

How much can I borrow for a payment I can afford?

Switch to “What can I borrow?” and enter your payment. A payment of $300 a month at 8.5% carries a loan of $14,622 over 5 years, $18,944 over 7 years and $24,196 over 10 years. The longer the term, the more a given payment borrows — but the more interest you pay in total, so the table under the result shows both.

Is a shorter loan better?

A shorter loan has a higher payment but costs far less interest. On $15,000 at 8.5%, 3 years is $473.51 a month with $2,046 of interest, 5 years is $307.75 with $3,465, and 7 years is $237.55 with $4,954. The comparison table under every result shows 1, 3, 5, 7 and 10 years for your own numbers.
